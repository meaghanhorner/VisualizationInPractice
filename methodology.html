Methodology

To determine exactly how much increased temperatures due to climate change would impact power demand:

First, I identified the most populated metro areas in the PJM service area: Baltimore, MD; Chicago, Il; Pittsburgh, PA; Philadelphia, PA; and Washington, D.C., and downloaded historical CDD (cooling degree days) at 65° and 75°

I used daily demand logs from PJM to collect daily electricity use data over the entire PJM service area.

Each metro area was assigned a weight based on its population. I used this weight to convert city-level monthly CDDs into a single regional temperature index reflecting cooling demand for the month across the entire region.

For future climate scenarios, I pulled long-term climate projections from NOAA for two possible scenarios: Moderate Warming (RCP 4.5) and High Warming (RCP 8.5).

Because NOAA's projections use a 65° baseline (CDD65) and higher grid stress occurs at elevated temperatures, I wanted to use a 75° baseline (CDD75). I used historic ratios of 65CDD to 75CDD to estimate projected CDDs at the 75° threshold.

Using the PJM historical demand data alongside the historical weather data, I calculated a 'cooling slope' which represents how much electricity demand rises as temperatures climb over 75°.

I plotted PJM's official estimated demand baseline (the grey dashed line). To isolate their implicit estimated cooling factor, ooling factor' I subtracted April demand (where cooling or heating costs are minimal) from July demand.

Finally, I took PJM's estimated July demand, subtracted my calculated cooling factor, and instead added in my estimated cooling factor, using my estimated CDD75 for the region and my calculated cooling slope.
